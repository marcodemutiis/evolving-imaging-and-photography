## The Screen Body Double

Today we are looking at avatars, digital doppelgängers and bodies who live on screen. 

### Robbie Cooper, *Alter Ego*, 2007

![Alter Ego]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Screen-Bodies/assets/img/cooper.jpg)
<figcaption>Robbie Cooper, from <em>Alter Ego</em>, 2007</figcaption>

<blockquote>In 2003 I was photographing the CEO of a company, when he told me that he used virtual world games to play with his children. He was divorced and had bad access to them, so he would meet them every evening in "Everquest", where they would play and chat. I asked him, what did they talk about? He told me that they discussed things like homework, school, their mother; the normal stuff of humdrum reality. His description of this banal but emotionally important exchange, taking place in the vivid fantasy of the game, got me thinking about the nature of the game itself; it's a world of surface appearances and symbols. Within that, their interaction had been reduced to text; it was a technological extension of psychological models- the imaginary, and the symbolic structure of language.

Reading the work of the economist Edward Castronova, it was already obvious that these surface appearances were rapidly achieving significant real world value, as items for sale between players. Castronova's 2001 paper "Virtual Worlds: A First Hand Account of Market and Society on the Cyberian Frontier" now seems quaint in how far it underestimated the rate of growth in this secondary market. But the level of demand was already climbing at an extraordinary rate. I spent the next three years, on and off, travelling to places like Korea, China, France and Germany to photograph people who use virtual world games. By recording the appearance of the real person, alongside that of their avatar, I wanted to compare each person with the identity that they'd created to interact with others online.

Source: Robbie Cooper, [artist website](https://robbiecooper.com/project/alter-ego)
</blockquote>

### [LaTurbo Avedon](https://www.laturboavedon.com/), [*ID*], 2015
![LaTurbo Avedon]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Screen-Bodies/assets/img/laturbo.png)
<figcaption>LaTurbo Avedon, still from <em>ID</em>, 2015</figcaption>


<blockquote>LaTurbo Avedon is an artist and avatar whose work gives new meaning to the term “born-digital” – as Avedon is a gender-neutral figure that originated in, and works within, virtual spaces. In those spaces, found in video works and animations, they question the role of digital authorship and the boundary between physical and virtual worlds.

Through their “constant state of character creation,” Avedon explores technology’s transformation of personhood. There’s no trace of the artist behind the avatar as Avendon doesn't see a distinction between the two. They say, “My experience and identity is only connected to what I have done online. As a render, I am making my character not much different than most people, sharing pics, making things, and chatting via social media.”

Their latest work “Sittin’ Up in My Room” (2018) is an animated image series whose voyeuristic invitations into Avedon’s bedroom complicate our limited perceptions of what it means to live on the digital plane. Throughout their video and animation work, Avedon takes visual cues from the 3D world of Second Life, having remarked that they spend more time playing video games than surfing the web. Their ongoing project “New Sculpt” uses different game development applications like Unity to propose a non-material way of creating and exhibiting sculpture. Avedon also maintains an active d.rip feed, where they post artwork and interact with followers.

And while Avedon makes their own art, their very existence online – where they curate shows and deejay in virtual clubs – can be seen as an extended performance or a thought-experiment that seeks to provide newfound meaning to the digital world as a valid space in which humans can exist and interact. “While you may not ever meet me walking through the park or on the street somewhere,” they say, “my experiences in ... virtual places are lived and legitimate.”

<p>Source: <a href="https://www.artsy.net/artwork/laturbo-avedon-id">artsy.net/artwork/laturbo-avedon-id</a></p> </blockquote>

### Robin Bervini, *Until I Try To Get Out Of My Skin*, 2020
![Robin Bervini]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Screen-Bodies/assets/img/bervini.webp)
<figcaption>Robin Bervini, from <em>Until I Try To Get Out Of My Skin</em>, 2020</figcaption>

<blockquote><p>Robin Bervini is the offspring of a bi-racial union. Being mixed race in Southern Switzerland, he always struggled in being perceived and accepted as Swiss. Because of this, growing up he has been negating his ethnicity to himself. He evaded his skin by embodying white avatars in video games, the only way he could live his white fantasy. Using that same strategy, this project takes the viewers into the artist’s skin trough a virtual reality experience and a spatial installation. After digitizing his entire body, the artist created alter-egos representing his ideal selves in different stages of life, while their identity is a manifestation of the artist’s own internalized racism, shame, social anxiety and lived discriminations.</p>

<p>The realization of this project involved the use of 3D scanning and photogrammetry techniques to digitize the artist’s body, and a series of processes to enable motion capture of facial performance and optimize the models as game-ready assets in order to create the virutal reality experience.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://www.robinbervini.com/until-i-stop-trying-to-get-out-of-my-skin"></a>robinbervini.com/until-i-stop-trying-to-get-out-of-my-skin</p></blockquote>

### [Lu Yang](http://luyang.asia/), *DOKU Trilogy*, 2018 – 2025 
![Robin Bervini]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Screen-Bodies/assets/img/yang.webp)
<figcaption>Lu Yang, still from <em>DOKU The Creator</em>, 2018 – 2025</figcaption>

<blockquote><p>Lu Yang’s ongoing DOKU trilogy of video works (2018–2025) explores the dissolution of selfhood in relation to digital, biological, and spiritual systems. Across DOKU The Self (2022), DOKU The Flow (2024) and DOKU The Creator (2025), the Chinese artist (*1984 in Shanghai, based in Shanghai/Tokyo) employs video game aesthetics, motion-capture technology, and Buddhist philosophy to interrogate where identity resides when consciousness can be rendered, replicated, and endlessly reborn.</p> 
<p>Throughout the trilogy an avatar based on the artist’s own likeness is seen to experience or inhabit various realities, ranging from mundane to the utterly fantastic. Inspired by ideas from Yogācāra Buddhism—particularly the concept of ālayavijñāna or “storehouse consciousness”—Lu Yang pictures DOKU not as a stable character but as a flickering assemblage of data, neurons, and spiritual concepts. Altogether, the trilogy asks whether the self, once decomposed into genetic code, brain activity, and algorithmic behavior, can be said to exist at all, or whether it persists only as impermanent patterns cycling through biological and digital networks.</p>
<p>Source: <a href="https://www.fotoarsenalwien.at/en/exhibition/lu-yang-en">fotoarsenalwien.at/en/exhibition/lu-yang-en</a></blockquote>

### Akihiko Taniguchi, *New Old Homeland*, 2025
![Akihiko Taniguchi]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Screen-Bodies/assets/img/taniguchi.jpeg)
<figcaption>Akihiko Taniguchi, still from <em>New Old Homeland</em>, 2020</figcaption>

<blockquote><p>For this project, I created a video artwork exploring the themes of the loss of one's hometown and identity. As with my past works, it was created using the game engine Unity, and it features an avatar generated from a 3D scan of myself. This piece was inspired by the various forms of "hometown" that we encountered throughout the Field Research Lab article series. Using memorable scenes and motifs from the interviews as clues, I expanded my imagination and constructed the scenes piece by piece. In addition to these newly created elements, the work also weaves in motifs from my past creations, such as recent music videos and two-dimensional artworks. While centered around this theme, I feel it also served as an opportunity to reorganize my thoughts from the past few years. Here, I would like to jot down—albeit in fragments—the things I remembered and pondered during the production process.</p>

<p>Source: <a href="https://okikata.org/%E2%98%83/newoldhomeland/">okikata.org/%E2%98%83/newoldhomeland/</a></p></blockquote>


### Yifan Li, [*What Is Your Favorite Primitive?*](https://drive.google.com/drive/search?q=yifan%20li), 2023
![Yifan Li]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Screen-Bodies/assets/img/li.jpg)
<figcaption>Yifan Li, still from <em>What Is Your Favorite Primitive?</em>, 2023</figcaption>

<blockquote><p>Li Yi-Fan is a contemporary artist who skillfully combines image-making technologies with narrative inquiry. He describes his creative process as a “death match” between the artist and software—a confrontation whose exhausted collision eventually gives rise to a quietly emerging narrative. Rather than relying on commercial tools, Li insists on building his own image-making systems from scratch, crafting a DIY yet technically advanced platform that rivals the capabilities of the resource-intensive digital effects industry. This autonomy reflects a distinct technological literacy and a deeply philosophical approach to image creation.</p>
<p>Li’s works often integrate game engines and real-time rendering technologies, allowing him to improvise intricate 3D animations with performative immediacy. What Is Your Favorite Primitive (2023), for example, takes the form of a satirical tech keynote presentation, in which a protagonist grapples with the ethical and societal questions embedded in software tools designed for image production. Through this work, Li reflects on how images reshape modes of communication and how emojis might transmit emotional meanings that surpass individual perception. At its core, the piece speculates whether image technologies might one day project an as-yet-unrealized totality—constructing a new politics of life and death.</p>
<p>Source: artist portfolio</p></blockquote>

### [Nina Davies](https://www.ninadavies.net/), [*Precusring*](https://artreview.com/art-lovers-movie-club-nina-davies-precursing-2023/), 2023
![Nina Davies]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Screen-Bodies/assets/img/davies.jpeg)
<figcaption>Nina Davies, still from <em>Precursing</em>, 2023</figcaption>

<blockquote><p>A little-known aspect of video games is that some serve as a training space for artificial intelligence. For example, some AIs that guide driverless cars have been trained using video games like Grand Theft Auto. In these simulated environments, pedestrians are NPCs (Non Playable Characters) whose movements standardise human gestures, eliminating differences between bodies, personalities or cultures.</p>

<p>This project by artist Nina Davies includes a video set in the near future in which two people talk about a pedestrian being hit by an autonomous car and the trial that ensues. In the conversation we discover the existence of a popular choreographic phenomenon called precursing in which people imitate the movements and gestures of NPCs in the hope of being identified by the AI of self-driving cars better, and thus avoid being run over. In Davies’ words, these people who imitate NPCs in public spaces are like ghosts between two worlds: they live in one of them, but have learned to communicate with the other.</p>

<p>Source: <a href="https://www.archivos.getxophoto.com/en/archive/2024-play/artists-2024/nina-davies/">archivos.getxophoto.com/en/archive/2024-play/artists-2024/nina-davies/</a></p></blockquote>
