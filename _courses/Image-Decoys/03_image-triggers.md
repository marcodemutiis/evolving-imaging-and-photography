This cluster explores how the automated logics of machine vision can be exploited, subverted, or turned against themselves—compromising algorithmic surveillance systems and disrupting the monetization of attention. Today, images are not merely captured; they are continually analyzed by software embedded in everything from smartphones and CCTV to doorbells and self-driving cars. Machine vision systems scan for faces, bodies, gestures, license plates—treating visual data as actionable information. Automated License Plate Recognition (ALPR) networks, used for traffic enforcement, toll collection, and law enforcement surveillance, represent a particularly pervasive form of computational seeing.


## Kate Rose, [*Adversarial Fashion*](https://www.katebertash.com/adversarial-fashion), 2020
![Kate Rose]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Image-Decoys/assets/img/rose.png)
<figcaption>Kate Rose, SITUATIONS/Strike Limited Edition T-shirt, 2020</figcaption>

Designer and activist Kate Rose has created a line of garments under the project Adversarial Fashion that interferes with these systems. The clothes feature carefully designed patterns that are interpreted by ALPR cameras as false license plates—feeding noise into the databases of surveillance networks and exposing their vulnerabilities. In 2020, commissioned by Fotomuseum Winterthur, Rose produced a limited-edition T-shirt emblazoned with a quote from artist and author Trevor Paglen, which became part of this adversarial tactic. The shirts not only mislead machine vision but embed critical reflections directly into the system database: “How do we see the world with machines? What happens if we think about photography in terms of imaging systems instead of images?... What are the implications of a world in which photography is both ubiquitous and, curiously, largely invisible?”

## Sanne Weekers, [*Anonymity Scarf*](https://www.instagram.com/p/C3BEpnVSUJn/), 2017
![Sanne Weekers]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Image-Decoys/assets/img/weekers.jpg)
<figcaption>Sanne Weekers, <em>Anonymity Scarf</em>, 2017</figcaption>

Other designers have explored how garments can disable or confuse machinic vision. In 2017, Sanne Weekers developed the Anonymity Scarf, printed with multiple faces that act as decoys, disrupting facial recognition systems. Similarly, fashion studio Cap_able creates textiles embedded with adversarial images—visual noise designed to deceive neural networks. These garments appear as colorful abstractions to human eyes but are misread by AI systems, rendering faces undetectable or wrongly categorizing people as objects or animals. Such image decoys protect biometric data and highlight the vulnerabilities of surveillance infrastructures. 

## [Cap_able](https://www.capable.design/), 2019 –
![Cap_able]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Image-Decoys/assets/img/cap_able.jpg)
<figcaption>Cap_able, Knit Long Sleeve Dress, screenshot from the Cap_able online shop</figcaption>

A more recent example is Digital Camouflage, a project by media artist Simon Weckert, who developed a garment intended to evade the computer-vision technologies increasingly used to monitor public spaces. To the casual observer, the shirt appears to feature an abstract, almost hypnotic design. Its pattern, however, serves a specific purpose: to disrupt object-recognition algorithms. These technologies are becoming part of urban surveillance infrastructure, including at Berlin’s Kottbusser Tor, while similar systems are already operating in cities such as London and Los Angeles.

Image triggers also emerge in the algorithmic economies of social media, where bots are programmed to seek out popular images for profit. In 2019, artists and illustrators began noticing that images posted on platforms like Twitter were rapidly scraped by automated scripts and printed on merchandise sold through third-party e-commerce sites—often without consent or attribution. These bots scan for images with high engagement (likes, retweets, comments) and phrases like “I want this on a shirt,” converting social capital directly into commodified goods.

## ["I Want This On A T-Shirt" Bot Bait](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/i-want-this-on-a-shirt-bot-bait), 2023
![Bot Bait Tweet]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Image-Decoys/assets/img/botbait.png)
<figcaption>Screenshot of a tweet by @hannadouken, 2023</figcaption>

In one notable online intervention, user @hannadouken tweeted an illustration captioned “This site sells STOLEN artworks, do NOT buy from them!” and invited others to bait the bots by replying with shirt-request comments. The tweet exploded, and within hours, her protest image was scraped and republished on T-shirts across online shops. Others joined in, flooding the bots with similar bait—sometimes using copyrighted material to trigger takedowns and legal challenges. By mimicking the behavior bots were trained to seek out, these users turned automated extraction mechanisms into unwitting participants in their protest.

In all these projects, images become triggers for algorithmic vision—they do not need to be actively decrypted by humans but are automatically activated by systems scanning for inputs. Through their representational surface, they bait machines and short-circuit the logic of the systems they inhabit. These images perform as decoys: designed to lure surveillance tools, data-scraping bots, and algorithmic viewers into engaging with content that undermines their purpose, revealing the blind spots and vulnerabilities of machine-seeing apparatuses.

## Assignment

Create your own *Image Triggers* project inspired by the examples seen in class. Choose one of two options:

**Option A: Test a trigger.** Pick a machine vision system you can access, such as a face-detection demo, an object detector, Google Lens, or an automatic captioning or alt-text tool. Design an image, pattern or intervention that tries to confuse, mislead or redirect how the system sees. Test several variants and document what happens, including when it fails. What did the system see, and what did it miss or misread?

**Option B: Set a bait.** Think of an automated process that looks for specific images, such as a scraper, a recommendation algorithm, an indexing bot or a print-on-demand script. Design an image or caption that works as bait for it, and describe how the system would respond and what that response would expose.

Whichever option you choose, remember that adversarial patterns often work only on specific models and can be surprisingly fragile. A trigger that fails is still a result worth sharing.

Add your name, a description of your project and some images to the [share document](https://docs.google.com/document/d/16iRfE4wmEdfgtDQ03-WwIJDvqvegzwPMfz8YotXb-Zk/edit?tab=t.ywqnvnlufn).
