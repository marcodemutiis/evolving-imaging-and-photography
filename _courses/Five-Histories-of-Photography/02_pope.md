In this lecture I follow a series of images of the pope to touch on key moments in the history of photography and the digital image. Technical and cultural at once, these moments mark the transformations of the medium since its inception.

Why the pope? I am not entirely sure myself. Perhaps because a papacy is long enough for an election and a death to be photographed under very different conditions. Perhaps because the Holy See is among the most conservative institutions in existence, and the last absolute monarchy in Europe, so that it tends to clash, visibly, with the fast shifts of photographic and digital culture. I hope this does not offend anyone of Catholic faith: the images connected to the pontiff serve here as examples for a reflection on a medium in constant flux.

The sequence is not strictly chronological. It follows five conceptual shifts: from the photograph as **trace**, to the photograph as **construction**, to the image as **circulation**, to the image as **attention**, and finally to the image as **statistics**. Each section introduces the image, explains the pope's role in it, and anchors the anecdote in a key text from photography and media theory.

## Pope Pius IX: The Photograph as Trace

![Pius IX]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Five-Histories-of-Photography/assets/img/pope-pius-IX.png)
<figcaption>Unknown photographer, <em>Pope Pius IX (Giovanni Maria Mastai-Ferretti)</em>, albumen print, 1860s?, © <a href="https://www.npg.org.uk/collections/search/portrait/mw148056/">National Portrait Gallery, London</a></figcaption>

Giovanni Maria Mastai-Ferretti (1792–1878), Pius IX, is generally described as the first pope to be photographed. He also had the longest reign of any pope after Saint Peter (1846–78), and it was a reign of political collapse. Elected with a liberal reputation, he fled Rome during the revolutions of 1848, returned as a conservative, and watched the papal territories absorbed into the new Italian nation state (1861; Rome fell in 1870). He ended his life as the self-declared "prisoner of the Vatican", the last of the ["king popes"](https://www.raicultura.it/storia/foto/2019/03/Papa-Pio-IX-fc308210-726d-4696-9bac-8a5318f95c2e.html).

The arrival of photography was a second controversy running alongside the political one. The medium was spreading quickly across Europe but was not welcomed everywhere, least of all in conservative circles. Painting had been the Church's own image technology for centuries, and a story told in Catholic popular media has a cardinal of the Pecci family refusing to be photographed for most of his life, on the grounds that painting was a far nobler way to present a human being.

Pius IX, by contrast, accepted the new medium and sat for the camera on many occasions. We do not know exactly when or by whom most of these portraits were made, but they circulated as cartes de visite: the [Royal Collection holds an 1862 example](https://albert.rct.uk/collections/photographs-collection/portraiture/pope-pius-ix-1792-1878) by Fratelli d'Alessandri, bought for Queen Victoria. For the art historian Vicente Jesús Díaz Burillo, [this photograph is a founding moment of the modern papacy](https://www.ull.es/revistas/index.php/accadere/article/view/4608): an institution that survives modernity by adopting technical image reproduction. The first photographs of a pope are therefore both a technological milestone and a moment of cultural adaptation.

The second image makes the point sharper.

![Post-mortem portrait of Pius IX]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Five-Histories-of-Photography/assets/img/pope-pius-IX.jpg)
<figcaption>Unknown photographer, <em>A Post-Mortem Portrait of Pope Pius IX</em>, 1878</figcaption>

The pope who first allowed his living body to be photographed is also among the first whose dead body was. At this moment the debate between painting and photography is about the past: what is the relation between a photograph and something that has been?

**Key text: Roland Barthes, *Camera Lucida* (1980).**

- **That-has-been (*ça a été*).** Barthes' core definition of the photograph is a certificate of presence: something or someone existed in front of the lens at a specific moment. Photography's truth is the truth of a trace. For a more detailed gloss, see this [annotated reading](https://csmt.uchicago.edu/annotations/barthescamera.htm).
- **Photography and death.** Because the moment is irretrievably gone, every photograph carries a small version of death. The person photographed becomes what Barthes calls the *spectrum*, a returning ghost. Barthes' own example is a portrait of a man awaiting execution, which makes him read "he is dead and he is going to die" at once. The post-mortem portrait of Pius IX is a literal version of this.

## Pope Benedict XVI: The Photograph as Construction

![Pope Benedict XVI]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Five-Histories-of-Photography/assets/img/pope-benedict-xvi.jpg)
<figcaption>Oliviero Toscani, from the <em>Unhate</em> campaign for Benetton, 2011</figcaption>

In November 2011 Benetton launched *Unhate*, a campaign of photomontages showing political enemies kissing on the lips: Barack Obama and Hugo Chávez, Benjamin Netanyahu and Mahmoud Abbas, and Benedict XVI with a leading Egyptian imam. The company presented it as a call for tolerance and "global love", a way of making visible how hatred grows from fear of the other. The Vatican denounced the pope image as an unacceptable provocation, an offence to the faithful and an example of advertising violating elementary respect in order to attract attention. Benetton withdrew it about an hour after the protest, as reported by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2011/nov/17/benetton-pope-kissing-ads) and by [CBC/AP](https://www.cbc.ca/news/world/benetton-pulls-ad-with-pope-kissing-imam-1.1089967).

Nobody believed this photograph, and it offended anyway. This is the first sign that images act regardless of whether they are true, a point to which we will return. It is also the moment where we can ask what the digital changed. Photographs had been retouched and montaged since the nineteenth century, but digital tools made manipulation seamless, cheap, and ordinary. Here the pope is a raw material: a recognisable, highly codified body that can be moved into any scene.

**Key text: William J. Mitchell, *The Reconfigured Eye: Visual Truth in the Post-Photographic Era* (1992).**

- **The end of photographic truth.** Analogue photography relied on a physical, causal link between the world and the image ("the camera never lies"), which gave photographs the status of objective evidence.
- **The digital shift.** Digital images are grids of discrete pixels rather than continuous analogue traces. They are therefore mutable, seamless to edit, and easy to synthesise from scratch. Mitchell calls this the post-photographic era, and it is a crisis of the real: where Barthes' photograph certifies, Mitchell's image only proposes.

## Pope John Paul II: The Photograph as Circulation

![Pope John Paul II vs Pope Francis]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Five-Histories-of-Photography/assets/img/pope-funeral.webp)
<figcaption>Crowds in St. Peter's Square in 2005 (top) and 2013 (bottom). The top image shows the procession of John Paul II's body after his death; the bottom image shows the first appearance of Pope Francis after his election.</figcaption>

In March 2013 NBC News tweeted a composite of two photographs of crowds in St. Peter's Square, eight years apart. The 2005 crowd holds up a scattering of phones; the 2013 crowd is a field of lit screens. "What a difference 8 years makes", the tweet said, and the image went viral. The story is [a little misleading, as HuffPost pointed out](https://www.huffpost.com/archive/ca/entry/viral-pope-election-photos-tweeted-by-nbc-news-are-misleading-p_n_2878146): the bottom photo does show the crowd welcoming the newly elected Francis, but the top one was not taken at a papal election at all. It shows the days after the death of John Paul II, when his body was carried across the square. The comparison put a mourning crowd next to a celebrating one and let the caption do the rest.

Notice the thread back to Pius IX. The death of a pope, once documented by a single post-mortem print, is now witnessed by a crowd in which everyone now is a photographer. And the comparison image is itself a networked image: it is copied, stripped of context, captioned, and passed on for what it communicates rather than for what it records.

**Key text: Daniel Rubinstein and Katrina Sluis, "A Life More Photographic: Mapping the Networked Image" (2008).**

Writing at the rise of Web 2.0 and early smartphone use, the authors argue that the digital image should not be seen as an immaterial copy of the analogue print but as something new: the networked image.

- **From memento to data stream.** Snapshot photography was print-oriented, private, and tied to special occasions. The networked image is fluid, endlessly reproducible, and oriented towards transmission. It is made with ubiquitous smartphones and becomes part of the banal flow of daily life.
- **Photography as social currency.** The value of an image shifts from its aesthetic quality or permanence to its use in communication, sharing, and self-presentation. Photographing becomes a performative, communal act.
- **The dissolution of the original.** Images exist to be edited, tagged, shared, and remixed. The art-historical fixation on a single authoritative print dissolves, and who controls an image becomes blurred as human and machine collaborate.

## Pope Francis: The Image as Attention

![Pope Francis]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Five-Histories-of-Photography/assets/img/pope-francis-garibotto.webp)
<figcaption><!-- TODO: caption --></figcaption>

In November 2020 an Instagram photograph of the Brazilian model Natalia Garibotto in lingerie received a like, one among many thousands. This one came from the official account of Pope Francis. The like was noticed in mid-November, about six weeks after the photo was posted, and removed the next day. A Vatican spokesperson told The Guardian that the Holy See could rule out that it had come from them, and that [they had turned to Instagram for explanations](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2020/nov/19/pope-francis-instagram-like-brazil-model-natalia-garibotto). Garibotto joked that at least she was ["going to heaven"](https://web.archive.org/web/20201121141913/https://www.wired.it/internet/social-network/2020/11/20/papa-francesco-like-modella-brasiliana/?refresh_ce=). The seduction of the image was so strong that even the most pious could not resist it.

A "like" is a transaction. Attention is given to an image, the power of seduction is quantified, and value is created through circulation. Here the like of @franciscus also reads as an act of validation convertible into a place in paradise: a form of networked indulgences for the online age?

![Pope Francis]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Five-Histories-of-Photography/assets/img/pope-francis-garibotto2.webp)
<figcaption><!-- TODO: caption --></figcaption>

It happened again about a month later, or, in the words of the Spanish site mag24.es, "here we go again". A like from @franciscus appeared on another half-naked model, this time on a private account, and a screenshot circulated as proof, in the early-internet tradition of "pics or it didn't happen". The Vatican announced another investigation, and Italian media began to speculate. [Was it a real like from the pope's entourage, a fake, a publicity stunt, a human error, a hack?](https://www.tgcom24.mediaset.it/cronaca/il-profilo-instagram-del-papa-mette-nuovamentelike-a-una-modella_26915133-202002a.shtml) The newspaper [*Il Giornale*](https://www.ilgiornale.it/news/cronache/nuovo-modella-sexy-giallo-sull-account-instagram-papa-1912006.html) wondered whether it was a well-made Photoshop stunt to boost the popularity of the model, who is a star on OnlyFans. 

The truth might never come out, and deep down I want to believe that @franciscus was also a victim of the seduction of images. Lead us not into temptation, and all – although I equally like the idea of [Chinese hackers hijacking the Pope’s account as a prank](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/07/28/us/politics/china-vatican-hack.html) after hiding their attack in a letter of condolences from the Vatican’s secretary of state.  But the truth is also irrelevant to what I want to argue. The speculations of the Italian press already give an accurate overview of who acts behind the surface of the networked image: hackers who can steal digital identities, Photoshop virtuosos with more or less ethical goals, influencers inventing strategies to grow their social capital, and tired social media managers and precarious communication interns who might forget to log out of the pope's account while admiring half-naked models on a coffee break. They are all part of the networked workforce of the attention economy. They are also part of the invisible infrastructure that lets images appear on our screens: rendering farms, undersea cables, satellites, servers, coltan mines.

Behind every seductive image that tempts us into giving a token of attention lies a network of submerged stakeholders and resources, a very material infrastructure with a growing impact on the global environment. The seduction of images is built on top of this planetary system of human and machinic agents.

**Key text: Jodi Dean, "Communicative Capitalism: Circulation and the Foreclosure of Politics" (2005).**

- **Circulation over meaning.** Dean argues that in networked media what counts is that a message circulates, not that it is heard or answered. Participation, expression, and inclusion are captured and monetised as contributions to the flow.
- **The "authenticity" trap.** We can read influencer photography through this lens: audiences crave real connection, so influencers mimic intimacy with bedroom mirrors, unretouched skin, and authentic selfies. But this is not intimacy; it is the capture of affect. The influencer turns emotional life and bodily likeness into a visual commodity, and vulnerability becomes an aesthetic strategy for algorithmic relevance and sponsorship.

## The Balenciaga Pope: The Image as Statistics

![Balenciaga Pope]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Five-Histories-of-Photography/assets/img/balenciaga-pope.jpg)
<figcaption>Pablo Xavier (Midjourney), <em>Pope Francis in a puffer jacket</em>, March 2023 <!-- TODO: verify credit and caption --></figcaption>

In March 2023 a user of the r/midjourney subreddit posted an AI-generated image of Pope Francis in a huge white puffer coat. A Twitter user shared it with a casual "OKAAYYY" and it went massively viral, with many people, who [wrote about it](https://www.garbageday.email/p/pope-in-a-coat), taking it to be real. The tell is the cup that looks like a Starbucks cup in his hand. As the writer Joel Golby put it in The Guardian, he thought he was immune to being fooled online until he saw the pope in a coat.

Journalist Ryan Broderick offers three reasons why it travelled so far. The casual way it was shared invited no scrutiny. Midjourney version 5 was simply very good, and the community had found that it excels at what looks like high-fashion photography. And the pope is strange and does strange things all the time: he has blessed a Lamborghini, so a big puffer coat is not hard to believe. Broderick's verdict: this is probably the first real AI-generated hoax, and the alarmist reaction was already predictable.

Compare it with Benetton in 2011. There the image was a montage of photographs and nobody was fooled. Here the image was never a photograph at all, and many were. And compare it with the like of 2020: that attention-grabbing mechanism of the networked image now feeds the generation of images itself.

**Key text: Hito Steyerl, "Mean Images" (2023).**

Steyerl critiques tools like DALL-E and Midjourney. Algorithmic generation shifts visual culture from photographic representation to statistical rendering based on masses of scraped online data.

- **Statistical averages, not representation.** A generative model does not capture reality as photography does, nor does it create from nothing. It computes a statistical "mean" of its dataset, so its images are patchworks of the most common data points.
- **The double meaning of "mean".** Beyond the average, these images are "mean" because they are extractive. They depend on the unpaid work of artists and the underpaid work of microworkers who tag data, and they often amplify the sexism, racism, and other biases of the historical data.

## The Pope Trajectory

Laid side by side, the images tell their own history of photography.

| Pope | Image | Photographic shift | Key text |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pius IX | Portraits, 1860s–1878 | The photograph as trace of a body | Barthes, *Camera Lucida* (1980) |
| Benedict XVI | Benetton *Unhate*, 2011 | The photograph as construction | W. J. Mitchell, *The Reconfigured Eye* (1992) |
| John Paul II / Francis | St. Peter's Square, 2005 and 2013 | The image as circulation | Rubinstein and Sluis (2008) |
| Francis | The Instagram like, 2020 | The image as attention | Dean (2005) |
| Francis | The puffer coat, 2023 | The image as statistics | Steyerl (2023) |

We begin with a body that was once in front of a lens and end with an image that comes from no body at all, only from the average of millions of other images. In between, the pope moves from being the subject of a photograph to being its raw material, its audience, its object of attention, and finally its statistical residue. Each shift does not replace the previous one. The trace, the montage, the crowd, the like, and the average all coexist in the images we encounter today, and the course will keep returning to this accumulation.
