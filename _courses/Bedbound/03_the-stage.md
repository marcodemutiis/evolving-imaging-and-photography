## The Stage – the bed as a place for self-construction and photographic production, a stage for “the girl online”.

Last week the screen entered the bed and turned the sanctuary into a battleground. But the screen does not only bring images into the bed to be consumed, it also carries them out. The bed has become a place of image production: a set, a studio and a backdrop. Airbnb listing photos, OnlyFans streams, YouTube videos filmed from under the duvet and bedroom pictures submitted to game shows like *Interior Motives* all use the photographed (and filmed) bed to construct an identity for an audience. Photographs of beds were staged before, but never by so many people, for so many viewers, and so continuously.

A useful frame is Erving Goffman's *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959), which divides social life into a front stage, where we perform for others, and a backstage, where the mask can come off – with the bedroom being the classic backstage. On the internet the distinction collapses, because the backstage itself is what gets performed, and the intimate is staged as intimate. Jia Tolentino makes a related argument about the online self in *Trick Mirror* (2019), where she describes the internet as a place that rewards constant self-presentation. The YouTuber Alice Cappelle describes what this feels like from inside the bedroom:

<blockquote><p>“I often joke that the bedroom is one of the few places where I actually put effort into my appearance. Makeup, hair, lighting, angles − it all has to be perfect, and for a reason. I’m a content creator, influencer, video essayist, person on the internet, and most of what ends up on my YouTube channel has been filmed in my bedroom. My safe space is suffering from overexposure. I might not leave the house on a filming day and probably won’t see anyone, but I’ll still put on my best performance for whoever’s on the other side of the camera – the fans, the haters, and the ones who just got lost in the algorithm.”</p>
<p>– Alice Cappelle, <a href="https://www.cccb.org/en/w/articles/online-bedrooms-the-disappearance-of-private-space">“Online Bedrooms: the Disappearance of Private Space”</a>, CCCB Lab, 20 September 2025</p></blockquote>


The bed is already the space where identities are created, rehearsed and formed – from childhood to teenage years, with the bedroom carrying the traces of every version of its inhabitant. The bed becomes a stage especially for the construction of a female identity, playing an important role in shaping girlhood even before the camera makes this space public. With the bed as a stage for internet representation, a messy, clean, cluttered or minimal aesthetic can be performed to signal a particular identity, each following its own economy, politics and ideology attached to online "girl beds".

The construction of the self as "the girl online" names a role that online culture hands to all of us: a surface onto which desires, expectations and algorithms are projected. Some of the works in this lesson accept the projection, and some use it tactically, as a way of gaining space and agency. In that sense, the stage is also a battleground, one where the fight is about who controls the image.

The works in this lesson start from the bedroom as a set, in the teenage portraits of Adrienne Salinger from 1995, and as a webcam stage, in Jennifer Ringley’s JenniCam from 1996, the first always-on bedroom camera. The bedroom then becomes a game, in the YouTube show *Interior Motives*. In the online bedroom videos of Thomas and Soda it becomes a stage under constant observation, and in Faccani's girl bedroom a space between imposed identity and agency. A section on the girl online then looks at the tactics that theory describes. The bed also becomes a stage for the paid performance of rest, from cam rooms to sleep streams and Instagram accounts. We end with the aestheticised bed as an instrument of power, in Vespignani's Airbnb listings from the occupied West Bank. The question running through all of them is this: when does the bed become a stage, who is the audience, and who writes the script?


### Adrienne Salinger, [*Teenagers in Their Bedrooms*](https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/adrienne-salinger-teenagers-in-their-bedrooms-photography-publication-spotlight-031125), 1995 / 2025

![Adrienne Salinger]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/salinger.webp)
<figcaption>Adrienne Salinger, from <em>Teenagers in Their Bedrooms</em>, 1995</figcaption>

Adrienne Salinger photographed American teenagers in their bedrooms in the 1980s and 1990s. She found them in gyms, parks, restaurants and shopping-mall restrooms in New York, Washington and California, photographed around two hundred of them, and selected forty portraits for the book *In My Room: Teenagers in Their Bedrooms* (1995). Each portrait was made with a large-format film camera and is paired with snippets from the long conversations she had with her sitters while they faced it.

The rooms in the portraits are full of things that teenagers use to try on identities: posters, magazine clippings, street signs, trophies. The bedroom is shown as a workshop of the self, which the sitter stages for one photographer and one lens. The book was reissued by D.A.P. in 2025, with 26 further prints from the archive, and the reissue invites a comparison with today. The smartphone has changed the bedroom from a container for a private inner world into a space constantly penetrated by online stimuli, where self-presentation is shaped by the awareness of being watched. Salinger herself wonders whether anyone would be let into teenagers' spaces today.

More info: [dwell.com/article/teenagers-in-their-bedrooms](https://www.dwell.com/article/teenagers-in-their-bedrooms-adrienne-salinger-photography-book-reissue-e6a5d5b2)


### Jennifer Ringley, [*JenniCam*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jennifer_Ringley), 1996–2003

![Jennifer Ringley]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/jennicam.webp)
<figcaption>Jennifer Ringley, stills from <em>JenniCam</em>, 1999</figcaption>

In April 1996, a year after Salinger's book, Jennifer Ringley, then a student at Dickinson College in Pennsylvania, installed a webcam in her dorm room and put its images online as JenniCam. The page refreshed with a new still every few minutes, and from June 1997 faster updates were available to paying visitors. After moving to Washington, D.C., she added further cameras, including one covering the bedroom. At its peak the site was reported to reach millions of visitors a day, and more than a hundred outlets covered it. She closed it on 31 December 2003, citing PayPal's new anti-nudity policy.

Where Salinger's teenagers pose for one photographer who then leaves, the camera here never leaves. Ringley said that she never felt a need to hide anything, and that "I'm still alone in my room, no matter what", yet an audience of millions, and a paid tier, turned the room into a stage and her into a celebrity: she appeared on late-night television, and clips from her spin-off show were included in MoMA's *Fame After Photography* (1999). JenniCam anticipates much of what follows in this lesson, from always-on bedroom cameras to cam rooms and sleep streams, in which being watched in bed becomes a form of work.

More info: [bbc.com/news/magazine-37681006](https://www.bbc.com/news/magazine-37681006)


### Ben Mora, [*Interior Motives*](https://www.youtube.com/@InteriorMotivesShow), 2025–

![Interior Motives]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/interiormotives.jpg)
<figcaption>screenshot from <em>Interior Motives #47</em></figcaption>

*Interior Motives* is a YouTube game show hosted by Ben Mora that began in 2025. Celebrity guests and internet personalities are shown photographs of bedrooms, sometimes also fridges and car interiors, submitted by viewers, mostly LGBTQIA+ people in the US. They have to guess the age, gender and location of the person who lives there from visual clues alone. Mora describes it as "GeoGuessr for gender, essentially". When the guess is revealed, the inhabitant's image and Instagram handle appear on screen so that viewers can look them up.

The show treats the bedroom as a portrait and the bed as evidence. Every room is read as a statement about identity, which is exactly the logic of the online bedroom in reverse: the audience decodes what the occupant has composed, consciously or not. Mora says the format works against neat categories and welcomes unpolished rooms, which sets it apart from the hypercurated personas of social media. Yet the game still turns private rooms into content, and the housing crisis shows up in the background of many of the pictures.

More info: [dazeddigital.com/life-culture/article/71124](https://www.dazeddigital.com/life-culture/article/71124/1/ben-mora-wants-to-see-your-bedroom-interior-motives-youtube-lgbtq-interview)


### Mackenzie Thomas, [*I'm At Home*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fccuzvnb8JA), 2026

![Mackenzie Thomas]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/thomas.jpg)
<figcaption>Mackenzie Thomas, still from <em>I'm At Home</em>, 2026</figcaption>

*I'm At Home* is a short film and performance piece in which Mackenzie Thomas turns her bedroom into a public stage. She recorded roughly 30 days of static security-camera footage inside her bedroom and condensed it into a 15-minute video. The piece deals with self-surveillance, the boundaries between private and public space, theatre and contemporary sexuality.

By framing the bed and the room as a permanent stage, the video turns ordinary domestic life into a monitored performance. The camera never leaves its position, so there is no backstage left: the room is both set and archive, and the person in it is both performer and watcher of her own life. Where Cappelle's bedroom is a studio that has to be made perfect before the camera is switched on, here the camera is always on, and the question becomes what it means to perform for the internet when nothing is ever off the record.

More info: [youtube.com/watch?v=fccuzvnb8JA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fccuzvnb8JA)


### Molly Soda, [*ROOM MAKEOVER! \*messy girl aesthetic inspired\* girl clutter ♡*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LpwvjpKrQJA), 2023

![Molly Soda]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/soda.jpg)
<figcaption>Molly Soda, still from <em>ROOM MAKEOVER! *messy girl aesthetic inspired* girl clutter ♡</em>, 2023</figcaption>

Molly Soda is an artist who has used her own bedroom and her own online presence as material for a long time. In this 15-minute video she gives her room a makeover inspired by the "messy girl" aesthetic, in the format of the YouTube room-makeover genre. Her Substack essay on the same theme is called ["Girl Clutter"](https://mollysoda.substack.com/p/girl-clutter), and her shared YouTube playlists, such as ["Making the Bed"](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLC1Ry_tD1kTUeKUHpppDtTPzaQWCFaOj4), collect further research from vernacular visual culture in which the bed is the subject.

Clutter is normally what happens when nobody is looking, and here it is arranged for the camera and named as an aesthetic. This is the opposite of the sanctuary, which depends on privacy, and it shows how a "natural" bedroom can be a deliberate composition. Messy, clean, minimal and cosy all become looks that can be adopted to signal a certain kind of identity, and each one can be copied and photographed.

More info: [mollysoda.substack.com/p/girl-clutter](https://mollysoda.substack.com/p/girl-clutter)


### Benedetta Faccani, [*Girl Bedroom*](https://www.researchcatalogue.net/view/4691042/4691041), 2026

*Girl Bedroom* is a master's thesis in Industrial Design at the Royal Academy of Art in The Hague (KABK), published on the Research Catalogue. Faccani traces the history of the girl bedroom from a private space of self-construction to a public place that extends bedroom culture into the digital age.

Her research focuses mainly on physical rooms, but also reflects on what happens to the girl and to bedroom culture when it moves online. During the pandemic, millions of young people had to let teachers, classmates and friends into their rooms through screens, virtual classrooms and video calls, and the bedroom became the backdrop for school, work and social life, causing the boundary between public and private to collapse entirely. Faccani asks if the girl now curates her room consciously, knowing it will be seen, and how this changes when the room stops being the one place in the house outside the public eye.

Faccani also reflects on her own position and the tension between critique and participation: many mainstream narratives about girls are shaped by consumer culture and patriarchal expectations. She asks why anything associated with girls has to be seen as frivolous, silly or unserious, and why femininity demands justification in the first place. Her conclusion is that this tension, between femininity and feminism, lightness and activism, is not a failure but a fundamental part of girlhood.

Read together with the Girl Online (below), the research points to a bedroom that is a place where identity is imposed (by a culture that tells girls what their room should look like and what a girl is) and a place where it can be claimed. The girl bedroom becomes a space that moves from imposed identity to agency, which is why this work could just as well belong to the Battleground.

More info: [researchcatalogue.net/view/4691042/4691041](https://www.researchcatalogue.net/view/4691042/4691041)


### The Girl Online

The idea that "we are all girls online" goes back to Alex Quicho's essay ["Everybody Is a Girl Online"](https://www.wired.com/story/girls-online-culture/), published in *Wired* in 2023. It looks at the frameworks that shape how we behave in digital spaces, in particular how many of our desires are projected onto us from outside, through algorithms, recommendations and social media. Calling this position "girl" is not a gender claim. It points to the mechanism that has long reduced people read as female to a role of projection, an empty vessel filled with other people's desires, and online we all occupy this position as soon as we want to be liked, seen and desired.

The interview "Being a Girl Online", with Charlotte Reuß and Sophie Publig, builds on Quicho's text and describes the girl online less as a person than as a set of tools, a practice that moves between agency and imposed identity:

<blockquote><p>“[...] we think of the Girl Online less as a person and more as a set of tools or a kind of practice. [...] So, rather than an identity, it’s more of a practice. [...] They take all the projections placed onto them — the expectations, the stereotypes, the aesthetics — and use them to their advantage, almost like a Trojan Horse.”</p>
<p>– Charlotte Reuß, in <a href="https://geremia1.substack.com/p/being-a-girl-online">“Being a Girl Online”</a></p></blockquote>


Reuß adds that no one can step outside these structures ("imagining ourselves somehow outside this system isn’t just unrealistic — it’s dangerously naïve"), and that the ambivalence is therefore the place to work from: gaming the algorithm, staying one step ahead of being captured by it. Publig gives an example in the account Bimbo University ([@nikitadumptruck](https://www.instagram.com/nikitadumptruck)), which explains global political and economic events in "girlified" language. It feeds the algorithm what it wants on the surface and reroutes the content underneath. The bedroom is the natural home of this tactic, because it is where the mask is put on.

The same tactical use of the girl online's visual language can be found in artistic work. In *Opsec and Beauty* (2019), [Addie Wagenknecht](https://medium.com/read-write-participate/skincare-and-opsec-forever-8d2f4f37b438) pairs YouTube beauty-product reviews with cybersecurity advice, and in [*The Bots*](https://0100101110101101.org/the-bots-greek-market-now-streaming-on-dis/) (2020), Eva and Franco Mattes use a light, personal register to talk about homophobia, neo-Nazism and disinformation in Greece.


### Micaela Carolan, from [*100 Empty Cam Rooms*](https://donotresearch.substack.com/p/micaela-carolan-100-empty-cam-rooms), 2025

![Micaela Carolan]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/carolan.jpg)
<figcaption>Micaela Carolan, from <em>100 Empty Cam Rooms</em>, 2025</figcaption>

The bed is also a stage in the most literal sense for the economy of cam workers and sex workers, who perform for the camera on and around it. Micaela Carolan's *100 Empty Cam Rooms* looks at this stage when nobody is on it. In the artist's words, it is "a speculative portraiture series composed from images of vacant webcam rooms. Without their occupants, decoration, lighting, props and traces of labor become inadvertent portraits of the people who performed there."

An empty cam room shows the set without the performer. It reminds us that the space exists to produce a certain kind of image, and that making that image is labour, in the same way that the naps of Warburton's animators were part of a working day. The bed is a workplace here, and the performance of intimacy is what it produces.

More info: [micaelacarolandesign.com](https://micaelacarolandesign.com/), [donotresearch.substack.com](https://donotresearch.substack.com/p/micaela-carolan-100-empty-cam-rooms)


### Sam Taylor-Johnson, [*David*](https://www.npg.org.uk/collections/search/portrait/mw73998), 2004

![Taylor-Johnson]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/taylor-johnson.jpg)
<figcaption>Sam Taylor-Johnson, installation view of <em>David</em>, 2004</figcaption>

In *David* (2004), Sam Taylor-Johnson filmed the footballer David Beckham asleep in a Madrid hotel room in a single take of 107 minutes, a work commissioned by the National Portrait Gallery in London. Early Big Brother live streams also found a surprise audience in their night-time footage of housemates sleeping. Emerging practices like sleep streams redefine the bed as a stage within the digital reaction economy. Streamers broadcast themselves asleep, and viewers watch for community and entertainment, as someone to fall asleep with. The sleepstream today has become a business model, with a different variation appearing on streaming platforms (see below).


### BBC News, [“Sleep streams: How Kai Cenat and others make money filming themselves sleep”](https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-66524539)

![Sleep streams]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/sleepstream.webp)
<figcaption>"Stanley's exasperated expressions at being woken up form a big part of the stream", BBC/StanleyMOV</figcaption>

<blockquote><p>“Other forms of sleep streams have started to emerge. Rather than paying streamers to sleep, viewers pay to keep streamers awake. By donating money, people can trigger loud noises, alerts, flicker lights, or make things happen in a room to disturb the streamer from their siesta. These videos are like a live-action video game, in which the streamer is simply trying to fall asleep and the players - or the viewers - are doing whatever they can to wake them up.”</p></blockquote>

Here, the sanctuary is turned inside out. The streamer StanleyMov says he makes enough from occasional sleep streams to pay his rent, and viewers can pay $95 to give him an electric shock through a bracelet. "Usually when I do sleep streams, it's to make YouTube videos and make content out of it afterwards, so for me getting zapped is basically just content for the YouTube channel," he told the BBC. Sleep, which Crary describes as an obstacle to the 24/7 economy, has become a product to sell to people who want to watch it, and a game for people who want to interrupt it. The sleeper performs, the audience holds the controls, and the bed is a stage on which rest is something other people can pay to take away.

## Interviews

Over the semester you will develop a project based on one of the topics explored in class. Just a reminder that the project has two components:

- **A written text.** This can be a conversation or interview with a photographer, designer, artist or author connected to the themes of Bedbound, or a written reflection that engages critically with their work.
- **An image-based output** related to the topics explored in class, in a form of your choosing: for example portraits of people watching Netflix in bed, custom pillow covers and printed bedsheets, a projection only visible from a bed, or a sleeping performance streamed on Twitch.

Now it's a good moment to start thinking about your writing assignment as well, and decide who could be a good subject for an interview – if you plan to submit a conversation rather than personal text.

The works in this lesson show that the person in the bed is rarely the only author of its image. The bedroom is staged for an audience, shaped by platforms, and read by others. An interview lets you ask the person who knows the stage best how it is built, and who they build it for.

### Who to talk to

Choose someone whose bed is complicated by different tensions or dimensions: a place of work, performance, self-construction, sickbed or battleground, barricade or sanctuary. It can be an artist or author or person from the lesson's themes, or someone who has a different relationship to their bed.

Pick someone you can meet, call or write to, and whose experience you do not already know in full. You do not necessarily need to interview an expert in an academic sense.

### Preparing the conversation

- **Ask for consent clearly.** Tell the person what the interview is for, where it will be shown ([permanentbeta.network](https://permanentbeta.network)) and who will read it. Ask whether you can record it, and whether they want to be named or anonymous. Anonymise or leave out whatever they ask you to.
- **Write open questions.** Prepare six to ten questions that begin with how, what or when, and avoid questions with a yes or no answer. Order them from the concrete to the abstract.
- **Ask about the bed, not only about the person.** Questions that begin with an object, a corner or a habit usually get better answers than general ones.
- **Prepare follow-ups.** The best questions often come from something the person has just said, so leave room to ask "can you say more about that?"

### Conducting and editing

There are three ways to conduct an interview, and each one suits a different situation.

- **By email.** Send a short, numbered list of questions and give the person a few days to answer. This is the easiest option when you work across time zones or the person prefers to write. The answers are often more considered, but less spontaneous. Plan a second round in which you follow up on what interests you most, because you cannot ask follow-ups in the moment.
- **By video call.** Use a call on Zoom or a similar tool, and ask permission before you record. Test the sound beforehand and ask the person to sit somewhere quiet. If they are willing, you can ask them to show you their bed or their room through the camera. Most tools can produce an automatic transcript, which you should always check against the recording.
- **In person.** Meet in a place where the person feels comfortable, ideally in or near the room you are discussing, and ask permission before photographing anything. Record the audio (a phone is enough), take a few notes, and listen more than you speak.

Afterwards, transcribe the conversation (or collect the written answers) and edit it for length and clarity. Keep the person's words and the order of the questions as honest as you can, and say in a short note that the text has been edited. Send the final text to the person before you submit it, so that they can correct mistakes or withdraw what they do not want to be public.

## The Stage – das Bett als Ort der Selbstkonstruktion und der fotografischen Produktion, eine Bühne für „the girl online“.

Letzte Woche ist der Bildschirm ins Bett eingedrungen und hat den Zufluchtsort in ein Schlachtfeld verwandelt. Doch der Bildschirm bringt nicht nur Bilder ins Bett, damit sie dort konsumiert werden, er trägt sie auch hinaus. Das Bett ist zu einem Ort der Bildproduktion geworden: zu einem Set, einem Studio und einer Kulisse. Airbnb-Inseratsfotos, OnlyFans-Streams, YouTube-Videos, die unter der Bettdecke gedreht werden, und Schlafzimmerfotos, die bei Spielshows wie *Interior Motives* eingereicht werden, nutzen alle das fotografierte (und gefilmte) Bett, um für ein Publikum eine Identität zu konstruieren. Betten wurden schon früher inszeniert fotografiert, aber nie von so vielen Menschen, für so viele Betrachter:innen und so ununterbrochen.

Ein nützlicher Rahmen ist Erving Goffmans *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959), das das soziale Leben in eine Vorderbühne, auf der wir für andere auftreten, und eine Hinterbühne einteilt, auf der die Maske abgelegt werden kann – wobei das Schlafzimmer die klassische Hinterbühne ist. Im Internet bricht diese Unterscheidung zusammen, weil gerade die Hinterbühne selbst aufgeführt wird und das Intime als Intimes inszeniert wird. Jia Tolentino vertritt in *Trick Mirror* (2019) ein verwandtes Argument über das Online-Selbst: Sie beschreibt das Internet als einen Ort, der ständige Selbstdarstellung belohnt. Die YouTuberin Alice Cappelle beschreibt, wie sich das aus dem Inneren des Schlafzimmers anfühlt:

<blockquote><p>“I often joke that the bedroom is one of the few places where I actually put effort into my appearance. Makeup, hair, lighting, angles − it all has to be perfect, and for a reason. I’m a content creator, influencer, video essayist, person on the internet, and most of what ends up on my YouTube channel has been filmed in my bedroom. My safe space is suffering from overexposure. I might not leave the house on a filming day and probably won’t see anyone, but I’ll still put on my best performance for whoever’s on the other side of the camera – the fans, the haters, and the ones who just got lost in the algorithm.”</p>
<p>– Alice Cappelle, <a href="https://www.cccb.org/en/w/articles/online-bedrooms-the-disappearance-of-private-space">“Online Bedrooms: the Disappearance of Private Space”</a>, CCCB Lab, 20. September 2025</p></blockquote>


Das Bett ist bereits der Raum, in dem Identitäten entstehen, erprobt und geformt werden – von der Kindheit bis in die Teenagerjahre, und das Schlafzimmer trägt die Spuren jeder Version seiner Bewohner:in. Das Bett wird besonders zur Bühne für die Konstruktion einer weiblichen Identität und spielt eine wichtige Rolle dabei, wie das Aufwachsen als Mädchen geprägt wird, noch bevor die Kamera diesen Raum öffentlich macht. Wird das Bett zur Bühne für die Darstellung im Internet, lässt sich eine unordentliche, aufgeräumte, vollgestellte oder minimalistische Ästhetik inszenieren, um eine bestimmte Identität zu signalisieren, jeweils mit der eigenen Ökonomie, Politik und Ideologie, die mit den Online-„Girl Beds“ verbunden ist.

Die Konstruktion des Selbst als „the girl online“ benennt eine Rolle, die die Online-Kultur uns allen zuweist: eine Oberfläche, auf die Wünsche, Erwartungen und Algorithmen projiziert werden. Manche der Arbeiten in dieser Sitzung akzeptieren die Projektion, andere nutzen sie taktisch, um Raum und Handlungsmacht zu gewinnen. In diesem Sinne ist die Bühne auch ein Schlachtfeld, auf dem darum gekämpft wird, wer die Kontrolle über das Bild hat.

Die Arbeiten dieser Sitzung beginnen beim Schlafzimmer als Set, in den Teenagerporträts von Adrienne Salinger aus dem Jahr 1995, und als Webcam-Bühne in Jennifer Ringleys JenniCam von 1996, der ersten dauerhaft eingeschalteten Schlafzimmerkamera. Danach wird das Schlafzimmer zu einem Spiel, in der YouTube-Show *Interior Motives*. In den Online-Schlafzimmervideos von Thomas und Soda wird es zu einer Bühne unter ständiger Beobachtung, und in Faccanis Girl Bedroom zu einem Raum zwischen aufgezwungener Identität und Handlungsmacht. Ein Abschnitt zur Girl Online beleuchtet dann die Taktiken, die diese Theorie beschreibt. Das Bett wird außerdem zur Bühne für die bezahlte Performance von Ruhe, von Cam-Rooms über Sleep Streams bis zu Instagram-Accounts. Am Ende steht das ästhetisierte Bett als Instrument der Macht, in Vespignanis Airbnb-Inseraten aus dem besetzten Westjordanland. Die Frage, die sich durch alle Arbeiten zieht, lautet: Wann wird das Bett zur Bühne, wer ist das Publikum, und wer schreibt das Drehbuch?

### Benedetta Faccani, [*Girl Bedroom*](https://www.researchcatalogue.net/view/4691042/4691041), 2026

*Girl Bedroom* ist eine Masterarbeit im Studiengang Industrial Design an der Königlichen Kunstakademie in Den Haag (KABK), veröffentlicht im Research Catalogue. Faccani zeichnet die Geschichte des Mädchenzimmers nach, von einem privaten Raum der Selbstkonstruktion zu einem öffentlichen Ort, der die *Bedroom Culture* (Schlafzimmerkultur) ins digitale Zeitalter ausweitet.

Ihre Forschung konzentriert sich vor allem auf physische Räume, fragt aber auch, was mit dem Mädchen und der Bedroom Culture geschieht, wenn sie online gehen. Während der Pandemie mussten Millionen junger Menschen Lehrer:innen, Mitschüler:innen und Freund:innen über Bildschirme, virtuelle Klassenzimmer und Videocalls in ihre Zimmer lassen, und das Schlafzimmer wurde zur Kulisse für Schule, Arbeit und soziales Leben, wodurch die Grenze zwischen öffentlich und privat vollständig zusammenbrach. Faccani fragt, ob das Mädchen sein Zimmer heute bewusst kuratiert, weil es weiß, dass es gesehen wird, und wie sich das verändert, wenn das Zimmer nicht mehr der eine Ort im Haus ist, der dem öffentlichen Blick entzogen ist.

Faccani reflektiert auch ihre eigene Position und die Spannung zwischen Kritik und Teilhabe: Viele Mainstream-Erzählungen über Mädchen sind von Konsumkultur und patriarchalen Erwartungen geprägt. Sie fragt, warum alles, was mit Mädchen verbunden wird, als frivol, albern oder unseriös gelten muss und warum Weiblichkeit überhaupt Rechtfertigung verlangt. Ihr Fazit lautet, dass diese Spannung zwischen Weiblichkeit und Feminismus, Leichtigkeit und Aktivismus kein Scheitern ist, sondern ein grundlegender Teil des Mädchenseins.

Zusammen mit der Girl Online (siehe unten) gelesen, verweist die Forschung auf ein Schlafzimmer, das ein Ort ist, an dem Identität aufgezwungen wird (von einer Kultur, die Mädchen vorschreibt, wie ihr Zimmer auszusehen hat und was ein Mädchen ist), und zugleich ein Ort, an dem sie sich zu eigen machen lässt. Das Mädchenzimmer wird zu einem Raum, der sich von aufgezwungener Identität zu Handlungsmacht bewegt, weshalb diese Arbeit ebenso gut zur Sitzung Battleground gehören könnte.

### The Girl Online

Die Idee, dass „wir alle Girls online sind“, geht auf Alex Quichos Essay [„Everybody Is a Girl Online“](https://www.wired.com/story/girls-online-culture/) zurück, der 2023 in *Wired* erschien. Er untersucht die Rahmenbedingungen, die unser Verhalten in digitalen Räumen prägen, insbesondere wie viele unserer Wünsche von außen auf uns projiziert werden, durch Algorithmen, Empfehlungen und soziale Medien. Diese Position „Girl“ zu nennen, ist keine Aussage über Geschlecht. Sie verweist auf den Mechanismus, der als weiblich gelesene Menschen seit Langem auf eine Rolle der Projektion reduziert, ein leeres Gefäß, das mit den Wünschen anderer gefüllt wird, und online nehmen wir alle diese Position ein, sobald wir gemocht, gesehen und begehrt werden wollen.

Das Interview „Being a Girl Online“ mit Charlotte Reuß und Sophie Publig baut auf Quichos Text auf und beschreibt die Girl Online weniger als Person denn als Werkzeugkasten, als eine Praxis, die sich zwischen Handlungsmacht und aufgezwungener Identität bewegt:

<blockquote><p>“[...] we think of the Girl Online less as a person and more as a set of tools or a kind of practice. [...] So, rather than an identity, it’s more of a practice. [...] They take all the projections placed onto them — the expectations, the stereotypes, the aesthetics — and use them to their advantage, almost like a Trojan Horse.”</p>
<p>– Charlotte Reuß, in <a href="https://geremia1.substack.com/p/being-a-girl-online">“Being a Girl Online”</a></p></blockquote>


Reuß ergänzt, dass niemand aus diesen Strukturen heraustreten kann („imagining ourselves somehow outside this system isn’t just unrealistic — it’s dangerously naïve“), und dass die Ambivalenz deshalb der Ort ist, von dem aus gearbeitet werden kann: den Algorithmus austricksen und dem Zugriff einen Schritt voraus bleiben. Publig nennt als Beispiel den Account Bimbo University ([@nikitadumptruck](https://www.instagram.com/nikitadumptruck)), der globale politische und wirtschaftliche Ereignisse in „girlified language“ erklärt. Er füttert den Algorithmus an der Oberfläche mit dem, was dieser will, und lenkt die Inhalte darunter um. Das Schlafzimmer ist der natürliche Ort dieser Taktik, weil dort die Maske aufgesetzt wird.

Dieselbe taktische Nutzung der visuellen Sprache der Girl Online findet sich auch in künstlerischen Arbeiten. In *Opsec and Beauty* (2019) verbindet [Addie Wagenknecht](https://medium.com/read-write-participate/skincare-and-opsec-forever-8d2f4f37b438) YouTube-Rezensionen von Beauty-Produkten mit Tipps zur Cybersicherheit, und in [*The Bots*](https://0100101110101101.org/the-bots-greek-market-now-streaming-on-dis/) (2020) sprechen Eva und Franco Mattes in einem leichten, persönlichen Register über Homophobie, Neonazismus und Desinformation in Griechenland.

## Interviews

Im Laufe des Semesters entwickelt ihr ein Projekt zu einem der Themen, die im Kurs behandelt werden. Zur Erinnerung: Das Projekt besteht aus zwei Teilen:

- **Ein schriftlicher Text.** Das kann ein Gespräch oder Interview mit Fotograf:innen, Designer:innen, Künstler:innen oder Autor:innen sein, die mit den Themen von Bedbound verbunden sind, oder eine schriftliche Reflexion, die sich kritisch mit deren Arbeit auseinandersetzt.
- **Eine bildbasierte Arbeit** zu den im Kurs behandelten Themen, in einer Form eurer Wahl: zum Beispiel Porträts von Menschen, die im Bett Netflix schauen, individuelle Kissenbezüge und bedruckte Bettlaken, eine Projektion, die nur vom Bett aus sichtbar ist, oder eine Schlaf-Performance, die auf Twitch gestreamt wird.

Jetzt ist ein guter Moment, um auch über eure schriftliche Arbeit nachzudenken und zu entscheiden, wer ein gutes Gegenüber für ein Interview sein könnte – falls ihr ein Gespräch statt eines persönlichen Textes abgeben möchtet.

Die Arbeiten dieser Sitzung zeigen, dass die Person im Bett selten allein für ihr Bild verantwortlich ist. Das Schlafzimmer wird für ein Publikum inszeniert, von Plattformen geprägt und von anderen gelesen. Ein Interview erlaubt euch, die Person, die die Bühne am besten kennt, zu fragen, wie sie gebaut ist und für wen.

### Mit wem sprechen

Wählt eine Person, deren Bett von verschiedenen Spannungen oder Dimensionen geprägt ist: ein Ort der Arbeit, der Performance, der Selbstkonstruktion, ein Krankenbett oder Schlachtfeld, eine Barrikade oder ein Zufluchtsort. Das kann eine Person aus Kunst oder Literatur oder aus den Themen der Sitzung sein, oder eine Person mit einem anderen Verhältnis zu ihrem Bett.

Wählt jemanden, den ihr treffen, anrufen oder anschreiben könnt und dessen Erfahrung ihr nicht schon vollständig kennt. Ihr müsst nicht unbedingt Expert:innen im akademischen Sinne interviewen.

### Das Gespräch vorbereiten

- **Fragt nach dem Bett, nicht nur nach der Person.** Fragen, die mit einem Gegenstand, einer Ecke oder einer Gewohnheit beginnen, führen meist zu besseren Antworten als allgemeine.

### Das Gespräch führen und bearbeiten

Es gibt drei Wege, ein Interview zu führen, und jeder passt zu einer anderen Situation.

- **Per E-Mail.** Schickt eine kurze, nummerierte Liste von Fragen und gebt der Person ein paar Tage Zeit zum Antworten. Das ist die einfachste Option, wenn ihr über Zeitzonen hinweg arbeitet oder die Person lieber schreibt. Die Antworten sind oft überlegter, aber weniger spontan. Plant eine zweite Runde ein, in der ihr nachfragt, was euch am meisten interessiert, denn im Moment selbst könnt ihr keine Anschlussfragen stellen.
- **Per Videocall.** Nutzt einen Anruf über Zoom oder ein ähnliches Tool und fragt um Erlaubnis, bevor ihr aufnehmt. Testet vorher den Ton und bittet die Person, sich an einen ruhigen Ort zu setzen. Wenn sie dazu bereit ist, könnt ihr sie bitten, euch ihr Bett oder ihr Zimmer über die Kamera zu zeigen. Die meisten Tools können ein automatisches Transkript erstellen, das ihr immer mit der Aufnahme abgleichen solltet.
- **Persönlich.** Trefft euch an einem Ort, an dem sich die Person wohlfühlt, idealerweise in oder in der Nähe des Raums, über den ihr sprecht, und fragt um Erlaubnis, bevor ihr etwas fotografiert. Nehmt das Audio auf (ein Handy reicht), macht ein paar Notizen und hört mehr zu, als ihr sprecht.

Transkribiert danach das Gespräch (oder sammelt die schriftlichen Antworten) und bearbeitet es hinsichtlich Länge und Klarheit. Bleibt so ehrlich wie möglich bei den Worten der Person und der Reihenfolge der Fragen, und weist in einer kurzen Notiz darauf hin, dass der Text bearbeitet wurde. Schickt den fertigen Text der Person, bevor ihr ihn abgebt, damit sie Fehler korrigieren oder zurückziehen kann, was nicht öffentlich werden soll.
