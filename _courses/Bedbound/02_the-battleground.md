## The Battleground – the bed colonised by labour, capital and the screen

Last week the bed was a sanctuary, and we saw how fragile that promise is. This week we look at what happens when the screen enters the bed. At what point does the place of comfort become a battleground? The device that soothes us, entertains us and keeps us company also makes us part of platform capitalism, and sleep, the most private of our activities, is increasingly colonised by digital labour. How do we negotiate a space in which rest and work no longer have a border?

A useful frame is Jonathan Crary's *24/7: Late Capitalism and the Ends of Sleep* (2013). Crary describes an economy of markets, networks and streams that never close, in which time is no longer divided into working hours and rest. In such a world, sleep is an obstacle: a stretch of time in which a person produces, consumes and is reachable by no one. Its erosion happens through stimulants, devices and the expectation of constant availability, and it is telling that streaming companies have openly described sleep as a competitor. Within this logic, the screen changes character. It starts as comfort and ends as an engine of media consumption, optimisation and efficiency, and the moment it crosses that line is hard to pinpoint.

That ambiguity is visible in bed rotting. Staying in bed for hours can be self-care, a justified recuperation from exhausting days. It can also be avoidance, a way of numbing ourselves that leaves us more depleted than before. Between recuperation and burnout, the same posture can mean opposite things. The bed also shapes the bodies that use it. It disciplines who counts as productive and who as idle, it carries gendered ideas about comfort, ambition and care, and it sustains whole economies of mattresses, sleep trackers and apps.

The works in this lesson follow rest as it is pulled into the economy. Caesar and Warburton show work seeping into rest, through the hammock and through the nap under the desk. The *Ideal Male Living Space* meme and Kostic show the bedroom itself being reorganised around work, as a stripped-down platform or as a purpose-built workstation. We end with a video about beds in video games, where virtual characters sleep, and perhaps rest, more than the people controlling or watching them. The thread that runs through all of them is a question about delegation: who gets to rest, and who or what rests on our behalf?


### Sofia Caesar, [*Workation*](https://vimeo.com/353834797), 2019

![Sofia Caesar]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/caesar.jpg)
<figcaption>Sofia Caesar, installation view of <em>Workation</em>, 2019</figcaption>

Sofia Caesar's text for *Workation* begins with a moment of learning a word. She was in Rio, at her mother's house, working in a hammock, when she searched on her phone for "workation", the odd merging of work and vacation. The results were stock images of white men working in beds, hammocks and on beaches, and she realised she was living one of those pictures. Her head grew heavy, and she let its weight go. Gravity slowly lowered her to the floor, and her phone fell from her hand. Lying there, she imagined the carpet of offices turning into beach sand, and waves arriving to carry away everyone's phones.

The piece captures how leisure has been absorbed into work, and how the images that sell this arrangement are standardised and narrow in who they show. The hammock, a classic emblem of rest, becomes a workplace, and the stock photograph tells us what a relaxed worker is supposed to look like. Against this image stands the body, whose fatigue and weight interrupt the performance and pull it down to the ground. The final fantasy, of waves washing away the phones, is a gentle wish for the tide to do what we cannot do ourselves: switch the devices off.

More info: [cargocollective.com/sofiacaesar/workation](https://cargocollective.com/sofiacaesar/workation)


### Alan Warburton, Sprites I-IV, 2016

![Alan Warburton]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/warburton.jpg)
<figcaption>Alan Warburton, still from <em>Sprites I-IV</em>, 2016</figcaption>

*Sprites I-IV* is a series of 3D-scanned self-portraits that grew out of Warburton's time in Beijing. In 2012 he was part of a small British team working inside a visual effects studio that Western blockbusters regularly used for outsourcing. At every lunch break, the crew of around a hundred Chinese artists took pillows from under their desks and napped, some at their workstations and some in the screening room. Then they worked for another six hours, until the studios in London, Los Angeles and Vancouver were ready to review what had been done overnight.

In the series, figure and ground are merged in the same chromakey green, a colour that every effects artist recognises as a blank to be replaced. This reflects a paradox of digital labour: for the image to look seamless and photoreal, both the artist and the apparatus must be made invisible. The art historian Marcus Verhagen reads the work as expressing the new forms of precarity and subjection that come with digital capitalism. Here the bed is reduced to a pillow under a desk, and sleep is scheduled around other time zones, which is exactly what Crary's 24/7 economy asks of the body.

More info: [alanwarburton.co.uk/sprites](https://alanwarburton.co.uk/sprites)


### Ideal Male Living Space (meme)

![Ideal male living space]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/ideal-male.jpg)
<figcaption>"Ideal male living space" meme</figcaption>

Most of us have probably come across the "ideal male living space" meme: a sparsely furnished room with a mattress on the floor, a lone laptop or PC setup, and perhaps a camping chair. The joke is strongly gendered, suggesting that men are too lazy to buy furniture or make their rooms look pleasant, and there is some truth in that. But the missing bedframe also reflects an economic precarity that reaches beyond gender. Frames are bulky, expensive, hard to move and tedious to take apart, so after several moves in a short time it makes sense to give up on them. Unstable housing forced a renegotiation of how rooms work, and bedrooms became rationalised, functional spaces optimised for a handful of basic activities, echoing the military ethos of things that can be dismantled quickly and are meant to be temporary.

Neglecting the bed is also a symptom of an economic system that values work and condemns rest. When domestic space serves mainly productivity, sleep is deprioritised and the bed becomes an afterthought. Seen beyond its humour, the meme shows how people cope with isolated, precarious conditions in ever smaller rooms that offer no boundary between resting and working, and this is why it reveals the politics beneath domestic space. The same image now circulates as an aspiration. Photos of near-empty rooms are shared as goals, millionaire founders are said to sleep on the floor, and TikTok accounts use generative AI to produce luxurious houses that still feature a mattress without a frame. The floor mattress has become a sign of entrepreneurial commitment that is supposed to outlast success, and the bedroom has taken over from the garage as the symbol of founder culture. In this version, austerity is coded as masculine ambition, and a comfortable bed as weakness.

More info: [nudapaper.com/the-ideal-male-living-space](https://nudapaper.com/the-ideal-male-living-space/)


### Filip Kostic, [*Bed PC*](https://filipkostic.computer/), 2021

![Filip Kostic]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/kostic.jpg)
<figcaption>Filip Kostic, <em>Bed PC 2</em> (2022), installation view at Scherben, Berlin</figcaption>

In 2021, Filip Kostic found that only a narrow gap separated his bed from his PC workstation, and he saw a chance to optimise. For many people the bedroom has become the new cubicle, and rather than grieving the loss of an ideal like work-life balance, Kostic built *Bed PC*: an arc of monitors mounted around the head of a black-framed bed. He first built it at home during the pandemic, when working spaces were pressing into leisure ones. Split keyboards sit on either side of the mattress, a small table attached to the frame holds the mouse, and a custom-built computer hums underneath. The setup lets Kostic, or any user, enter every digital space without leaving the bed. In performances he staged livestreams of up to 24 hours fuelled by energy drinks, playing *World of Warcraft* while watching YouTube. At the gallery version he streamed on Twitch for eight hours, moving between the game *Risk of Rain 2*, a video call with a friend on Discord and the YouTube series *Art Thoughtz* by Hennessy Youngman.

Critics have read *Bed PC* as an image of capitalism reaching into every part of daily life, dissolving the boundaries between work, art, play and rest. A life once divided into separate spheres of activity becomes a single continuum of experience. Nominally recreational virtual spaces may offer some relief, but they can also keep alive the reality we hope to escape by helping us bear it a little longer. Whether it is a subversive or a sincere embrace of our work-life collapse, one reviewer observes, the work seems likely to produce a state of depressive insomnia tied to the corrosive effects of capital and overstimulation. Here the bed is fully absorbed into infrastructure, and the body at its centre is the part that has to be kept running.

More info: [Terminal Surrender](https://absdigitalart.substack.com/p/terminal-surrender), [The Brooklyn Rail](https://brooklynrail.org/2022/07/artseen/Klammern-aus-denen-Bltter-Sprieen-II/)


### Top 10 Video Game Lists, [“Get Some Sleep! It's the Top 10 Bed's In Video Game's!”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZhYFUw0EsFk), 2026

This is not an artwork, but it makes a useful case study. A content creator counts down the ten best beds in video games, in the spirit of the listicles that have driven much of the online attention economy. In many games, beds are mechanics: places where a character recovers health, saves progress or skips the night. The video treats them as objects of desire and ranks them like products.

The curious detail is who gets to rest. The game characters seem to sleep more, and more efficiently, than the YouTuber who lists their beds, and perhaps more than we do, watching the video in bed instead of sleeping. Rest has been turned into a stat, a feature to optimise and a form of content. The avatar has a proper bed, while the person behind the screen stays awake to watch. This is the logic of delegated rest, where our digital selves sleep better than our physical ones.

More info: [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/best-bedroom-setups-video-gaming/)
