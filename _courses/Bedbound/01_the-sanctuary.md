## The Sanctuary – what the bed promises: rest, privacy, dreams

We tend to think of the bed as a personal sanctuary: a safe place to withdraw from the world, to rest, to be alone with our thoughts, or to escape into dreams. It promises privacy, and sometimes something close to purity, with boundaries that feel almost sacred: no dirty clothes, no food, no devices.

This promise is more recent and more fragile than it seems. The private bedroom, with a bed that belongs to one person or one couple, is a relatively modern arrangement; for much of history beds were shared among family members, servants and guests. Today the sanctuary is also unevenly distributed, since it depends on having a home, a room and the security to close a door. When people are displaced from the bedroom, whether by homelessness, precarious housing, exhaustion or the demands of work, bodies improvise. They build and reclaim chairs, benches, pavements and other environments never planned for rest, and turn them into beds.

Even inside the bedroom, the sanctuary is porous. Self-care in bed increasingly overlaps with the image space of the screen: resting and consuming images merge into a contemporary break from productivity and efficiency, in which we indulge in scrolling and binge-watching on the mattress. The bed is also where we are most exposed. Asleep, we are defenceless and depend on trust, and a camera pointed at a sleeper turns that vulnerability into an image. At the same time, the bed is the setting for intimacy, desire and care, a place where bodies can be affirmed, or protected, outside public norms.

The works in this lesson move across these tensions. We begin with beds opened up to others (Calle, Goldin, Tillmans), where the private becomes shared, intimate and sometimes public. We then turn to beds without bedrooms (Chodzko, Jaszczuk), where rest is displaced into streets and public transport by exhaustion and necessity. We end with a bed rebuilt from digital scans (Hampe), where loneliness and comfort are experienced through devices. Throughout, keep three questions in mind: who is allowed into the bed, who is allowed to look at it, and what happens when the sanctuary breaks down?


### Sophie Calle, [*The Sleepers*](https://fraenkelgallery.com/portfolios/the-sleepers), 1979

![Sophie Calle]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/calle2.webp)
<figcaption></figcaption>

Sophie Calle's work tests the boundary between public and private life. In 1979 she had begun following strangers through Paris as a way of giving her days a structure. For *The Sleepers*, she brought this watching indoors. Over eight days she invited friends, acquaintances and strangers to take turns sleeping in her bed in eight-hour shifts, so that it was never empty. Nearly thirty people agreed to take part. When a guest did not turn up, Calle hired a stand-in or took the place herself. She photographed the sleepers at regular intervals, noted their gestures and habits, served meals and changed the sheets. What drew her, she explained, was not the wish to know someone's life but an interest in small details, such as which way a person sleeps or which side of the bed they choose. She later developed the project into an artist's book, a format central to her practice.

The bed here is a private space that is lent out rather than given up. Calle becomes host, caretaker and observer at once, and the project sits uneasily between hospitality and surveillance. Sleep is the moment of greatest vulnerability, and strangers are asked to trust someone with it. The work also sets up a link to Chodzko's film of the same name, discussed below.

More info: [MoMA](https://www.moma.org/calendar/galleries/5887)


### Nan Goldin, [*The Ballad of Sexual Dependency*](https://www.artlimited.net/agenda/nan-goldin-the-ballad-of-sexual-dependency-exhibition-gagosian-london/en/7586898), 1973–86

![Nan Goldin]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/goldin.jpg)
<figcaption>Nan Goldin, <em>Dieter on the bed, Stockholm</em> (1984), from <em>The Ballad of Sexual Dependency</em>, 1973–86</figcaption>

![Nan Goldin]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/goldin2.jpg)
<figcaption>Nan Goldin, <em>Empty beds, Boston</em> (1979), from <em>The Ballad of Sexual Dependency</em>, 1973–86</figcaption>

Made over thirteen years, *The Ballad of Sexual Dependency* is generally considered Nan Goldin's central work. It gives a frank account of gender, intimacy, desire and power, rooted in her own experience. Goldin does not photograph as an outside observer. She works from inside her relationships, recording friends, lovers and the communities around her with an emotional directness that had few precedents at the time, and in doing so turns personal experience into a shared record. The work began as a slideshow with a varied, carefully chosen soundtrack, which gave it an almost operatic, performative quality. It was first shown in New York nightclubs and alternative venues before Aperture published it as a book in 1986. Goldin's first book, it has been reprinted more than twenty times and remains one of the most influential photobooks ever made.

In this series the bed is a space of queer intimacy. The bedroom is where relationships that fall outside dominant norms could be lived, and where they could be photographed without being staged for an outside gaze. The two images shown here put presence and absence side by side: a person on a bed, and beds left empty. Together they suggest that the bed holds both the intimacy and its aftermath.

More info: [moma.org/calendar/exhibitions/1651](https://www.moma.org/calendar/exhibitions/1651)


### Wolfgang Tillmans, [*Today is the First Day*](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/wolfgang-tillmans-interview-brussels-1798353), 2020

![Wolfgang Tillmans]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/tillmans.webp)
<figcaption>Wolfgang Tillmans, <em>Kammerspiele</em>, 2016.</figcaption>

![Wolfgang Tillmans]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/tillmans2.webp)
<figcaption>Wolfgang Tillmans, <em>FKK naturiste</em>, 2008.</figcaption>

*Today Is the First Day* was Wolfgang Tillmans's retrospective at WIELS in Brussels, his first major institutional show in Belgium. Speaking about his intimate, personal pictures, Tillmans described them as an affirmation of everyone's basic freedom to decide over their own body, and he spoke of feeling privileged to live his homosexuality without fear of repression. He has also said that he photographs close to his own life not to talk about himself but to convey what it feels like to live today, and that keeping a visual record is never neutral, because what gets recorded and preserved is always a political choice. The exhibition title carries an optimistic sense of moving forward and starting anew, although Tillmans notes that it comes from a song he wrote in 2016, in a personal moment of freeing himself. His installation method matters too: photographs are taped or pinned to door frames and radiators, and recent and older works hang side by side as contemporaries.

The bed is not a declared subject of Tillmans's work, but it is close to everything he photographs. It is the place where the freedom to control one's own body is first exercised, and where the kind of intimacy that runs through his pictures usually takes place. His informal hanging also recalls how we pin images to the walls of our own rooms, which brings the private logic of the bedroom into the gallery. Read this way, the images invite us to ask how personal and queer intimacy can be made visible without being exposed, and what it means to share such moments in public.

More info: [RTBF](https://www.rtbf.be/article/wolfgang-tillmans-today-is-the-first-day-premiere-retrospective-du-photographe-allemand-au-wiels-10424169)


### Adam Chodzko, [*Sleepers*](https://vimeo.com/189273601), 2016

![Adam Chodzko]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/chodzko.png)
<figcaption>Adam Chodzko, still from <em>Sleepers</em>, 2016</figcaption>

*Sleepers* is built from a collection of amateur 35mm slides showing people asleep, which Chodzko has been gathering since 2007. The collection now numbers more than a hundred images, taken around the world between the 1950s and the late 1980s. They show everyone from sleeping babies to the elderly, and people dozing on buses, planes, boats, sofas and beds, on blankets outdoors, in wartime during the Vietnam War, and homeless on the street. The film imagines the restless movement of a mind as it flickers between perceptions, drifting towards unconsciousness, resisting it, or searching urgently for a way into sleep. It also plays on two kinds of uncertainty: looking at a sleeping person, we cannot be sure it is only sleep and not death, and looking at a still image, we wonder whether we saw it move.

Assembled together, the images suggest a collective narcolepsy, perhaps a response to exhaustion from ever more constant labour and the pressure to be present, alert and competitive. Chodzko evokes a pandemic of unconsciousness, a mass sleep with shared dreams, and offers it as a kind of hyperstition. He also reminds us that Hypnos, the Greek god of sleep, is the twin of Thanatos, the god of death, and that both are children of Nyx, the Night. Perhaps our waking reality is itself a form of sleep, a paralysis or hypnosis that keeps us from seeing the world clearly enough to act in it. For this lesson, the key point is that the bed is redefined as a space of rest that exists outside the bedroom, produced by very different conditions: homelessness, cultural attitudes to public space, and the exhaustion of contemporary life.

More info: [adamchodzko.com/works/sleepers](https://www.adamchodzko.com/works/sleepers/)


### Pawel Jaszczuk, [*High Fashion*](https://paweljaszczuk.com/High-Fashion-1), 2018

![Pawel Jaszczuk]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/jaszczuk.png)
<figcaption>Pawel Jaszczuk, from <em>High Fashion</em>, 2018</figcaption>

*High Fashion* grew out of photographs Pawel Jaszczuk made between 2008 and 2010 of drunk salarymen asleep on the streets of Tokyo. Living in Japan at the time, he would cycle out after midnight, finding that the hours between one and four in the morning suited his work best. Soon after he started the series in 2009, he published a black-and-white zine, *salaryman*, with Morel Books, in an edition of 150 copies that sold out immediately. For *High Fashion* he chose images in which the sleepers' poses echo those of fashion models, and designed the photobook as a glossy magazine, which explains the title. Jaszczuk insists the work is not a statement against drinking. He first wanted to capture the contrast between men in good suits and dirty pavements, but his view of the work has shifted over the years. With the Zen Foto Gallery publication, he hopes it will read as a humorous, apocalyptic vision of an overworked, overstressed society.

Here the street becomes a bed for exhausted workers, and the images move between fashion photography and documentary, and between public and private. They also open an ethical grey zone. The sleepers cannot consent, and the glossy styling raises the question of whether it dignifies them or exploits a moment of vulnerability. Compare this with Calle, where the sleepers agreed to be watched, and with Chodzko, where the images were found and gathered. Who has the right to look at, and to publish, a person asleep?

More info: [shashasha.co/en/book/high-fashion](https://www.shashasha.co/en/book/high-fashion)


### Elisa Hampe, *Through My Hands Broken Into Fingers*, 2026

![Elisa Hampe]({{ site.baseurl }}/courses/Bedbound/assets/img/hampe2.jpg)
<figcaption>Elisa Hampe, <em>Emma, Resting</em>, from <em>Through My Hands Broken Into Fingers</em>, 2026</figcaption>

Elisa Hampe's series looks at how loneliness, comfort and longing are experienced today through digital devices. She assembles scenes of isolated figures who rest in states of waiting and exhaustion, in domestic interiors where objects, bodies and spaces merge into one another. To make the images she developed a photogrammetry workflow. She builds the set on location and scans models, textiles, objects and interiors with her smartphone camera. She then turns these fragments into 3D assets and combines them digitally into larger compositions inspired by traditional painting, which gives the images shifted perspectives and a quality close to painting.

The workflow is central to the meaning of the work, because it changes surfaces in a distinctive way. Blankets lose their softness and become rigid shells, while bodies look hollow and fragile. In the software, objects can be endlessly manipulated yet stay frozen in their original positions, so Hampe can force elements into proximity without changing their form. Errors and distortions from the scanning process are kept in the images, which adds to the instability already present in the scenes. The result is a picture of mediated proximity and loneliness, closely linked to the phenomenon of bed rotting, in which people stay in bed for hours, scrolling and withdrawing, as a form of rest and escape. It is also the technique you will use in this week's assignment.

More info: [elisahampe.com](https://elisahampe.com/)

## Assignment

### Read

Dincer, D. (2022). [Beds without Rooms: Sleeping Rough](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/joid.12219). *Journal of Interior Design*, 47(3), 11–28. [https://doi.org/10.1111/joid.12219](https://doi.org/10.1111/joid.12219)

As you read, ask what a bed keeps of the sanctuary once it leaves the bedroom, and what it loses.

### Make

Taking Elisa Hampe's workflow as a starting point, scan a bed using photogrammetry or Gaussian splatting. "Bed" is open to interpretation, and you can work in one of two directions, in dialogue with the reading:

- **A bed as sanctuary:** your own bed or one you know, in whatever form you choose, including details such as a pillow, a fold or a stain.
- **A bed out of place:** a bench, a blanket on the floor, a car seat, or a makeshift bed you build yourself from everyday materials.

Experiment with details, lighting, reflections, cameras and 3D software. Soft fabrics and reflective surfaces make scanning difficult, but, as in Hampe's work, errors and distortions can become part of the image. Turn your 3D models into a series of 3 to 5 images (2D renders or views).

Scan only unoccupied beds and places you have permission to use, and keep people and faces out of your images.
